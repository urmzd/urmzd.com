Within the last couple of decades, computers, cellphones and the internet became fundamental tools that shifted society in ways no one expected. That shift is happening again — and if you're not learning, you're being left behind.

This isn't meant to scare you. It's a reminder of something you should've already been doing: question, research, validate, repeat. Don't wait for the perfect moment. Don't wait for someone to hold your hand. If you want people to help you, help yourself first.

## Question

Learning starts with earnest questions — questions without assumptions. The moment you assume, you introduce bias. Are you invested in a specific answer, or are you actually seeking the truth?

Think about it: you're a tired student who woke up at 6am for club practice, barely ate, barely slept, but somehow finished the essay you've been grinding on for weeks. Your classmate is reviewing it before final draft. What do you *want* to hear when you ask for feedback?

Exactly. You've shifted from curiosity to desire. Work from first principles — always. Strip away your assumptions. What truths remain? Stay curious.

Then there's the matter of ego. You can't be afraid of sounding like a fool. The uncomfortable reality is that if you're genuinely challenging yourself, you *will* sound like you don't know what you're talking about — because you don't. That's the whole point. Courage isn't asking questions when you already know the answer. It's asking when you don't.

This is true of anyone who's ever learned anything. The quality of your questions only improves if you keep asking them and reflecting on what comes back. A great question satisfies your curiosity — and often opens the door to even better questions.

## Research

Research is where most people get stuck. They don't even know where to start. If you've ever struggled with something, felt like you exhausted every option, and then heard "Google it" — you probably wanted to explode. Maybe someone even sent you a link that still didn't answer your question.

Here's why: most people are busy. They can't walk you through every possible solution and the path to get there. You're expected to derive the answer yourself. This has been true since the day you were born — the answers to life aren't handed to you. Research will eventually give you the resources to find the answer, but it rarely hands it to you directly.

This is where LLMs change the game. In the past, learning to research meant first researching "how to research" — confusing, right? If you had a great teacher or a helpful librarian, they might have taught you the basics. LLMs sidestep this entirely. They act as a thinking partner with broad domain knowledge — they're exceptionally good at helping you *start*.

That said, LLMs are faulty — just like any other source. Search engines surface what's popular, not what's true. Books reflect their author's biases. LLMs are no different; they're probabilistic by design, and they'll give you what you're most likely looking for. Which brings up a deeper problem: you'll find what you look for. If you go in with an assumption baked into your question, the answer will usually confirm it. That's not research — that's just feeling validated.

This is where personas become useful. If you prompt an LLM to think like a skeptic, a domain expert, or someone with a completely different background, you shift the lens it's operating through. Combined with the inherent randomness in how these models generate responses, you'll get meaningfully different takes on the same problem. You're not looking for the one right answer — you're stress-testing your question from multiple angles before you decide what to believe.

There are many ways to research, but here's my current approach:

1. I open my favourite LLM in my favourite browser
2. I enable my handy connectors/tools/plugins
3. I first prompt the LLM to think about the question, and solely output the facts without any commentary
4. I then prompt it to return a few different interpretations of the question and the answer, from various personas

## Validate

Validation is hard. My math teacher in my senior year of high school introduced me to the "known/unknown" matrix/framework.
This was popularized by US Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld

<BlockQuote author="Donald Rumsfeld" source="DoD News Briefing, February 12, 2002">
Reports that say that something hasn't happened are always interesting to me, because as we know, there are known knowns; there are things we know we know. We also know there are known unknowns; that is to say we know there are some things we do not know. But there are also unknown unknowns—the ones we don't know we don't know. And if one looks throughout the history of our country and other free countries, it is the latter category that tends to be the difficult ones
</BlockQuote>

The idea here is that there's going to exist things we don't know we don't know. The only way to build a validation framework is through experience.
Yes, it's difficult. You do this everyday in many ways, you learn from experience. Validation requires understanding the problem you're trying to solve, and what requirements must be met to satisfy in order to confirm it.
Each domain has different rules, and each project, each question has a different set of requirements. It's often on you to define these requirements in highly specific scenarios.

While in math, validation can be performed by tracing the logic - in science, the experiment can be reproduced, in business - you might need to collect data, have metrics in place - it all depends. Hence, why experience is important, exposure to previous problems will enable you to tune existing frameworks, and build off them.

## Repeat

Often, you'll need to refine your approach, ask a more specific question, before performing slightly more research, and then get to your answer. Much like how evolution is a byproduct of the environment; learning and growth is a byproduct of curiosity. In order to find the best framework, you'll often need to traverse the vast wealth of knowledge. Sometimes, you'll miss the answer, or only get a part of it - but over time you'll improve your heuristics, you'll learn what works and what doesn't - and you'll ultimately become more knowledgeable.

## On Getting Help

People don't want to be treated like a tool — they want to feel a connection. But connection has a prerequisite: demonstrated effort.

When you ask someone for help without having done the work first, you're handing them the full cognitive load — your confusion, your research, your problem-solving. Most people won't take that on for a stranger. It's not cruelty, it's energy management.

But show up having already tried — with a specific question, a failed approach, some context — and something shifts. Gouldner, building on Malinowski, offers a useful explanation in his work on the [norm of reciprocity](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2092623): helping isn't really about goodwill, it's structural. Each party in a social exchange has both rights and duties, and the exchange only functions when both sides are participating. When you skip the effort, you haven't offended anyone — you've just stepped outside the machinery entirely. There's no contract to activate, so none does.

This isn't a manipulation tactic. It's just honest: doing the work first changes what you're asking for. You're no longer asking someone to solve your problem. You're asking them to close the gap — and that's a much easier yes.

#### The Math

**Minimax** — The value of a game state depends on whose turn it is:

$$
V(s) = \begin{cases} \text{eval}(s) & \text{if } s \text{ is terminal or depth} = 0 \\ \max_{a} V(\text{next}(s, a)) & \text{if maximizing} \\ \min_{a} V(\text{next}(s, a)) & \text{if minimizing} \end{cases}
$$

**GP Fitness** — A genome's fitness is its win rate across a tournament of games:

$$
f(g) = \frac{\text{wins}(g) + 0.5 \cdot \text{draws}(g)}{\text{games}(g)}
$$

#### The Code

```python
import random

EMPTY, X, O = 0, 1, -1
CENTER, CORNERS, EDGES = 4, [0, 2, 6, 8], [1, 3, 5, 7]

def winner(b):
    lines = [[0,1,2],[3,4,5],[6,7,8],[0,3,6],[1,4,7],[2,5,8],[0,4,8],[2,4,6]]
    for l in lines:
        s = b[l[0]] + b[l[1]] + b[l[2]]
        if s ==  3: return X
        if s == -3: return O
    return EMPTY

def moves(b):
    return [i for i in range(9) if b[i] == EMPTY]

def evaluate(b, genome):
    cw, kw, ew = genome
    score = 0
    for i in range(9):
        if b[i] == EMPTY:
            continue
        w = cw if i == CENTER else kw if i in CORNERS else ew
        score += b[i] * w
    return score

def minimax(b, depth, is_max, genome):
    w = winner(b)
    if w != EMPTY: return w * 100
    if not moves(b) or depth == 0: return evaluate(b, genome)
    fn = max if is_max else min
    return fn(
        minimax(
            b[:m] + [X if is_max else O] + b[m+1:],
            depth - 1, not is_max, genome
        )
        for m in moves(b)
    )

def best_move(b, player, genome):
    is_max = player == X
    fn = max if is_max else min
    return fn(
        moves(b),
        key=lambda m: minimax(
            b[:m] + [player] + b[m+1:],
            3, not is_max, genome
        ),
    )

def play(g1, g2):
    b = [EMPTY] * 9
    genomes = {X: g1, O: g2}
    turn = X
    for _ in range(9):
        m = best_move(b, turn, genomes[turn])
        b[m] = turn
        w = winner(b)
        if w != EMPTY: return w
        turn = -turn
    return EMPTY

def evolve(pop_size=30, gens=20):
    pop = [[random.uniform(-1, 1) for _ in range(3)] for _ in range(pop_size)]
    for gen in range(gens):
        fits = []
        for g in pop:
            wins = sum(
                1 for opp in random.sample(pop, min(5, len(pop)))
                if play(g, opp) == X
            )
            fits.append(wins)
        ranked = sorted(zip(fits, pop), reverse=True)
        top = [g for _, g in ranked[: pop_size // 3]]
        pop = []
        while len(pop) < pop_size:
            p = random.choice(top)
            child = [w + random.gauss(0, 0.1) for w in p]
            pop.append(child)
        best = ranked[0]
        print(f"Gen {gen:>2}: best fitness={best[0]}, genome={[f'{v:.2f}' for v in best[1]]}")
    return ranked[0][1]

print("Evolving tic-tac-toe heuristics...")
best = evolve()
print(f"\nBest genome: center={best[0]:.2f}, corner={best[1]:.2f}, edge={best[2]:.2f}")
```

#### The Connection

Grid search tries every combination of weights — brute force over the entire space. GP iterates through selection pressure: generate, evaluate, select the best, mutate, repeat. It's the same loop as the blog's framework. You don't need to search the entire space — you need a good feedback loop.
