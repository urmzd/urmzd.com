The introduction of computers, cellphones and the internet became tools that shifted society in ways no one expected. That shift is happening again - however the stakes are now higher. It becomes far too easy to become reliant on these tools, and slowly lose your critical thinking capabilities. This blog isn't meant to scare you, it's a reminder on the importance of the critical thinking loop: question, research, validate and repeat. 

## Question

Learning starts with earnest questions. Are you invested in a specific answer, or are you seeking the truth? You've probably been in a situation where you've placed all your hope into getting a specific answer. You might have asked someone on a date, or feedback on your project, maybe even the mechanic the status of your car . In all of these situations, you've probably just wanted the positive, the yes, the great, the amazing. Expectations will almost certainly cause disappointment. Always work from first principles. Once you strip away your assumptions, what truths remain?

Then there's the matter of ego. You can't be afraid of sounding like a fool. The uncomfortable reality is that if you're genuinely challenging yourself, you *will* sound like you don't know what you're talking about. That's the whole point. Courage isn't asking questions when you already know the answer. It's asking when you don't. This is true of anyone who's ever learned anything. The quality of your questions only improves if you keep asking them and reflecting on what comes back. A great question satisfies your curiosity — and often opens the door to even better questions.

## Research

The next step is not a step, but rather a process - one that many people get wrong. In most cases, people will fall into one of two buckets. First, they explore pieces of information until a belief is confirmed. Alternatively, people often extrapolate their own thoughts, effectively side-stepping any meaningful exploration. In defense of these people, very rarely does anyone want to spend massive amounts of time collecting and retaining information without a goal in mind. Academic researchers spend years looking into various sources as part of a broader goal. I'd be hard-pressed to find people who would spend all those years if they knew that their research would produce no meaningful results. It's why there's exists a not so insignificant problem in academia, [publication bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_bias).

 Research meant potentially taking hours, days, weeks or even years as you dove into various sources. LLMs have changed this - we're able to move faster than we have at any other point in history. LLMs act as a thinking partner with broad domain knowledge — they're excellent at helping you gather information from several different locations and output them in an easily digestable format. That said, LLMs are faulty — just like any other source. Search engines surface what's popular, not what's true. Books reflect their author's biases. LLMs are no different; they're probabilistic by design and the providers hosting them bake in system prompts to fit their internal philosophies or internal guidelines. Which brings up a deeper problem: you'll often get the "safe answer". If you go in with an assumption baked into your question, the answer will usually confirm it. That's not research — that's just feeling validated. This is where personas become useful. If you prompt an LLM to think like a skeptic, a domain expert, or someone with a completely different background, you shift the lens it's operating through. Combined with the inherent randomness in how these models generate responses, you'll get meaningfully different takes on the same problem. You're not looking for the one right answer — you're stress-testing your question from multiple angles before you decide what to believe. Another important thing to note is that LLMs are not created equally. Different entrypoints will provide you different perspectives. The unfortunate truth, providing a good prompt still requires you to learn how to prompt. By the very nature of the companies that provide them to the public - they're designed for broad use-cases, and hence are limited by design.

## Validate

Validation is hard. There's no other clearer way to state that. Even established methods like the scientific method have issues that continually need to be revised upon and improved. There isn't a true validation framework in the real world for complicated multi variable problems. To be precise, any problem in an open-system will never have a guaranteed solution (and hence why the next step is so important). There is no way to validate with absolute confidence that any solution is guaranteed to be the answer. Reliable knowledge at scale will always require independent verification from several different parties with conflicting interests. Learning rarely produces guarantees, instead it helps you build confidence and establish a threshold for how wrong you're willing to be.

## Repeat

Often, you'll need to refine your approach, ask a more specific question, before performing slightly more research, validate it and then ask more questions before you get an answer you're satisfied with. Much like how evolution is a byproduct of the environment; learning and growth is a byproduct of curiosity. In order to find the best framework, you'll often need to traverse the vast wealth of knowledge. Sometimes, you'll miss the answer, or only get a part of it - but over time your questions improve, your research improves and you establish reasonable validation thresholds. You'll learn what works and what doesn't - becoming more knowledgable in the process. You'll need to repeat the loop until the cost of new information surpasses the aquisition cost.

## On Getting Help

People don't want to be treated like a tool — they want to feel a connection. But connection has a prerequisite: demonstrated effort.

When you ask someone for help without having done the work first, you're handing them the full cognitive load — your confusion, your research, your problem-solving. Most people won't take that on for a stranger. It's not cruelty, it's energy management.

But show up having already tried — with a specific question, a failed approach, some context — and something shifts. Gouldner, building on Malinowski, offers a useful explanation in his work on the [norm of reciprocity](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2092623): helping isn't really about goodwill, it's structural. Each party in a social exchange has both rights and duties, and the exchange only functions when both sides are participating. When you skip the effort, you haven't offended anyone — you've just stepped outside the machinery entirely. There's no contract to activate, so none does.

This isn't a manipulation tactic. It's just honest: doing the work first changes what you're asking for. You're no longer asking someone to solve your problem. You're asking them to close the gap — and that's a much easier yes.
