The greatest strategists and philosophers to exist throughout history had one ability that enabled them to make extraordinary decisions under pressure.

It was the ability to sh... I'm not talking about opening up a terminal and creating a quick shell script:

```bash
#!/usr/bin/sh
echo "Shh..... it's storytime!"
```

Though there will be a time and place for that.

## The Quiet Ones

<PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius">Marcus Aurelius</PreviewLink> led Rome through plague, war, and political betrayal — one of the most turbulent periods in the
empire's history. His strategy wasn't louder speeches or faster action. It was separation: splitting
what was happening externally — the pace, the chaos, the pressure — from what happened internally —
the decisions, the silence.

In his _Meditations_, he wrote:

<BlockQuote author="Marcus Aurelius" source="Meditations, 4.3">
  A man can retire to no more a quiet place than to his own soul; he especially who is beforehand
  provided of such things within, which whensoever he doth withdraw himself to look in, may
  presently afford unto him perfect ease and tranquility.
</BlockQuote>

In other words, the silence within one's mind offers a place to understand what is inside — and what lies ahead of you outside.

Nearly two thousand years later, on September 26, 1983, a Soviet lieutenant colonel named <PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislav_Petrov">Stanislav Petrov</PreviewLink> sat in a bunker outside Moscow when the early warning system reported that the United States had launched nuclear missiles. Protocol demanded he report it immediately — which could have triggered a retaliatory strike. Instead, he paused. He reasoned that a real first strike would involve hundreds of missiles, not five. He waited for corroborating evidence. None came. It was a false alarm caused by a system malfunction. His silence — the decision to _not_ act — prevented a nuclear war.

Two men, separated by nearly two millennia, practicing the same discipline: the ability to be still when everything around them demanded noise.

## The Impulse to Act

The ability to remain silent in loud environments is a difficult skill to master. Humans want to be validated, it's a biological impulse to be seen and heard. We want to be part of the conversation, to contribute, to be recognized. Left unchecked, this impulse can lead to noise - the type of noise that dampens other signals, the type of noise that makes it harder to see clearly.

The noise usually comes in the form of thoughts that won't stop, "I haven't said anything in a while, maybe I should say something", "I have an idea, but it's not fully formed yet, should I share it?", "I don't want to be seen as quiet or unengaged, maybe I should speak up". 

Though, it also comes in the form of actions, "I'm going to do this thing to showcase X, Y or Z" and then it spirals, one action leads to another and you find yourself having done a whole lot of nothing to prove nothing.

If that's you, then you also need to recognize that it's completely normal. It's our social default speaking to us. We want to have a place in the hierarchy that we've built in our minds. A mapping of our social worth against that of others, often created without any awareness.

<PreviewLink client:load href="https://openpublishing.princeton.edu/read/social-hierarchy-power-status-and-influence/section/a7b8910b-625c-437a-8f68-46e3f9cd214d">Social Hierarchy, Power, Status, and Influence</PreviewLink>

We'll do anything at any moment, and commit to an action to justify our position in the hierarchy we've magically created. 
This is where it gets interesting... If we place ourselves in a hierarchy, and we're unaware of it, what does that say about how we value ourselves, and others?

## Awareness of Values

The lack of awareness is the root cause of both impulsive behavior and overthinking...

Tim Pschyl has an amazing series called Solving thhe Procrastination Puzzle where he explores this in depth. Here's a link to the first part: <PreviewLink client:load href="https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2014/10/procrastination-puzzle">Solving the Procrastination Puzzle</PreviewLink>

</Aside>

Don't blame yourself for it though, it's a natural consequence of our evolutionary past. Our brains process millions of different pieces of information at any given moment, and being aware of all of it at once would cause an entirely different issue, <PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_paralysis">action paralysis</PreviewLink>.

During our hunter-gatherer days, action paralysis would almost certainly mean death. So we learned to react based on learned patterns. Likewise, not having a position in the hierarchy might mean that you were disposable. Reactivity and status-seeking weren't flaws — they were survival strategies.

But the progression of society has changed that. While society as a whole has encouraged less reactivity, it doesn't necessarily mean that our brains have magically changed with it. We're 10,000 years past the days of hunting, but our brains are still stuck there. 

We need to recognize the defaults that exist within us, unwrap the abstractions and pay attention to the impulses that drive us to do act without thought, or think and never act. How do we solve this problem then?

We need to learn how to prune the different choices ahead of us, learn how to build heuristics that work for us, and implement safeguards to prevent overthinking and decision paralysis. 

In the eyes of a data scientist: avoid exploring too many branches in the decision tree, and don't overfit the model — not every feature is critical in making a decision. For a regular person, it means you might need to take a breath in between transitions, and slow down the moment before a decision is made. You don't have superpowers, but what you do have is time.

<Aside>

A decision tree is a way of taking a set of characteristics about something and arriving at a yes or no answer.

Say we want to answer: **is Urmzd likely to be a Software Engineer?**

Here's what we know about him:

- Urmzd is a martial artist
- Urmzd likes math
- Urmzd studies computer science
- Urmzd loves Nutella

Given enough examples of people like this, you'd start to figure out which characteristics actually matter — and you'd be fast at it.

Why? Because you'd prune. Martial arts and a love for Nutella don't tell you much. You'd zero in on the two characteristics that do: the program he's in, and a appreciation for math.

</Aside>

## Decisions, Not Choices

<PreviewLink client:load href="https://fs.blog/about/">Shane Parrish</PreviewLink>, in his book *Clear Thinking*, goes into the importance-time tradeoff, or as he called it, the ALAP
(As Late As Possible) principle — the more important the decision, the more time you should take
before making it. Note how I'm using the word *decision* and not *choice*:

<PullQuote>A decision is intentional, a choice is spontaneous.</PullQuote>

It's not about being a fool or a true leader — it's about risk and reliability. Are you someone people can depend on, or are you a liability? That's how people tend to make assessments, whether they realize it or not. In organizational psychology, <PreviewLink client:load href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/258792">Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman's widely cited model of trust (1995)</PreviewLink> identifies three factors people use to evaluate trustworthiness: perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity — with predictability as a behavioral signal that reinforces all three. Unpredictability disrupts that signal, and hence, impulsivity is seen as a risk. In professional and competitive environments, it positions you on a negative trajectory for growth — because the people around you are pruning. When they're at the cusp of something great, they minimize risk.

<BlockQuote author="Shane Parrish" source="Clear Thinking">
  Defaults can transform caution into an excuse not to act if you don't resist them. Anyone who has
  held on to a failing job, relationship, or investment too long knows that information gathering
  reaches a point of diminishing returns — at some point the cost of getting more information is
  exceeded by the cost of losing time or opportunity.
</BlockQuote>

In that specific quote, the focus is on the consequence of waiting too long — but the opposite is also true. There can be little to no return if enough information isn't gathered before acting.

But recognize one important detail: at no point did I define what "impulsive" really means. By now, you've probably built a mental image of someone — maybe me — speaking up to ask a ridiculous question. But at that very moment, you made an impulsive decision of your own. You chose what it meant before it was defined, before the information was given.

And that assessment isn't always fair. By reflexively shutting down those who seem to be acting impulsively, you cut off more than noise — you potentially lose simple solutions that work off the bat, or valid approaches that someone picked up as a natural skill. There's more to consider than what's immediately visible. Curiosity is the key: ask why they think that, revisit the idea later, give an avenue to be unconventional. The difference between a reckless impulse and a sharp instinct is often just context — and you won't know which one it is if you prune before you look.

And this cuts both ways. We often act impulsively ourselves — especially when we have experience. The "quick decisions" that come with expertise can feel earned, but they still need to be reassessed. <PreviewLink client:load href="https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amr.35.4.zok579">Erik Dane's research on cognitive entrenchment (2010)</PreviewLink> shows that as domain expertise deepens, mental schemas become increasingly rigid — the very patterns that made you effective start to limit your flexibility. You get faster, but not necessarily better. We can improve at making decisions, but we have to recognize that at some point, we too will become the comfortable ones — the ones operating on autopilot, pruning ideas out of habit rather than judgment. Every generation of experts eventually becomes the old guard, and the cycle repeats.

## The Ego in the Ring

I saw this play out constantly as a Muay Thai coach. The gym is one of the environments with the most ego per square foot. You'd get CEOs, athletes, professionals — people who are used to being the authority in their world. Then they walk in and get humbled by the difficulty of the class and they can't keep up, or they hit too hard in a drill only to find out the other person can hit _significantly_ harder.

These people tended to drop out the earliest. It was too difficult for them — maybe physically, but more so for their sense of self. They didn't progress. They'd come in occasionally and try to showcase their skills to the newer members, an attempt to self-validate their position in a hierarchy that nobody else was keeping track of.

But then there's the soft, quiet 14 year old kid that comes in. People around them wreck them at first — bigger, stronger, more coordinated. And the kid just keeps showing up. Trains every day. Treats every round, every correction, every loss like a lesson. Two years later, that same kid is outperforming their peers, their coaches and after each session, still asking questions, still learning, still improving. They don't care about the hierarchy — they just care about the work. They don't care about being the best — they just want to be better than they were yesterday.

Being a full-grown adult and having your ass handed to you by a 16-year-old with less experience feels awful. And to say experience doesn't matter isn't true — it does. But the number of years doesn't. People conflate social worth with duration or achievements — how long you've been somewhere, how many years you have on someone, how many things you've accomplished. In Muay Thai, none of that matters. While it might matter in your social circle surrounded by your echo chamber, it almost certainly doesn't the moment you step out.

Awareness and vulnerability go hand in hand. To be aware is to recognize that you're going to hurt yourself — forced to confront things you didn't want to. You're not where you thought you were? You're not as smart as you believed? All of these things hurt. But what hurts longer is never learning, and gate keeping the growth of both yourself and of others. The ego is the biggest obstacle to growth, and the moment you let it go, you can start to see clearly.

## Breaking the Hierarchy

The point isn't that thinking you're above someone is bad and thinking you're below them is good. Both are limiting. The moment you place yourself anywhere on a ladder relative to someone else, you stop seeing clearly. You either stop listening because you think you already know, or you stop contributing because you think you have nothing to offer.

Great leaders understand this. They understand the challenges, they understand the domain — and they encourage thought and behavior while letting people learn through real challenges. While it would be great to say anything in a fully open environment, the reality is that the companies and people like to move fast and if you can't meet people's expectations, you'll get cut off.

But long term, in my opinion, it helps no one. It creates an echo chamber — the same thought process, the same way of thinking, recycled instead of challenged. You optimize too early. You prune before you understand what actually matter. And there's a tendency for people to use the internal hierarchy and the shame they feel as a way of contributing to this cycle instead of breaking it. Shame is something I'd like to explore a bit more in a future post, but for now, shame is a composite emotion that arises from the guilt of not meeting an internalized standard, combined with the fear of being judged by others. It's a powerful motivator, but more often than not, it leads to people becoming protective of their position in the hierarchy, rather than being open to learning and growth. It creates a feedback loop where people are more concerned with avoiding the truth than with seeking truth or improvement.

The fix isn't to remove all structure — it's to create boundaries, as any healthy activity should, while encouraging genuine lessons. One practical example: split a meeting into two parts. A brainstorm session where people get their thoughts out, and a working session where the ideas are defined and refined. Changes and new directions can happen at another time. This goes beyond software — it can be applied to any decision-making process.

## Separate the Thinking from the Doing

In Muay Thai, the best individuals would focus on one or two — maybe on a good day, three — moves, techniques, or strategies per session. Micro-adjustments would happen throughout the sparring and fights, but the focus was always on execution. Brainstorming and revision were separate activities. There's no way to perfect a kicking technique while you're being punched in the face.

And here's something counterintuitive: when it comes to practice rounds, you don't always want the most competitive partner. The best practice partners aren't necessarily the most skilled. They're the ones who react in ways you might not have thought about, and they're not so far ahead of you that you can't _play_ — which is arguably the most important part of any learning activity.

Play is where the noise goes silent. It's where you stop performing for the hierarchy and start exploring for yourself. It's where you try a technique you've never thrown, ask a question you thought was stupid, or test an idea that hasn't been validated. It's the one space where everything is quiet except the activity in front of you.

That's what silence really is. Not the absence of sound — the absence of the noise that keeps you from seeing clearly. That's what it means to 'sh', quiet the noise, and separate the thinking from the doing. It's not about being quiet for the sake of being quiet — it's about creating a space where you can see clearly, and then acting on it.

## `sh`

In 1971, <PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Thompson">Ken Thompson</PreviewLink> wrote <PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thompson_shell">the first Unix shell</PreviewLink> — a simple command interpreter that let users talk to the operating system. It was called `sh`. Six years later, <PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_R._Bourne">Stephen Bourne</PreviewLink> rebuilt it at Bell Labs, adding variables, control flow, and the scripting capabilities that would become the foundation of modern Unix. The Bourne shell shipped with Version 7 Unix in 1979, and its descendants — Bash, Zsh, Dash — power nearly every Linux and macOS system today.

But here's what's interesting about `sh`: it's silent. When it runs well, you don't hear from it. It doesn't announce what it's doing. It doesn't ask for validation. It takes an input, processes it, and produces an output — only when asked. No noise, no ego, no performance. Just execution.

There's a stereotype of the Linux user who writes a script that fixes everything. You never hear about them. They don't show up in the stand-up to explain their approach. They don't need credit for the architecture. They just get it done — quietly — and move on.

That's the `sh` in all of this. Not silence for the sake of silence, but silence as a tool. The discipline to separate the noise from the signal, the brainstorm from the execution, the hierarchy from the work. Marcus Aurelius retreated into his own mind to lead an empire. Petrov waited in a bunker while alarms screamed. The 14-year-old kept showing up and training while everyone else was performing.

<PullQuote>
  In the terminal, `sh` is how you execute. In life, "shh" is how you prepare to.
</PullQuote>

#### The Foundation

- <PreviewLink client:load href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_tree">Decision Trees</PreviewLink>

#### The Math

**Entropy** — Measures the impurity or uncertainty in a dataset:

$$
H(S) = -\sum_{i=1}^{c} p_i \log_2(p_i)
$$

**Information Gain** — How much a feature reduces uncertainty when splitting:

$$
IG(S, A) = H(S) - \sum_{v \in \text{values}(A)} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|} H(S_v)
$$

#### The Code

```python
from math import log2
from collections import Counter

def entropy(labels: list[str]) -> float:
    counts = Counter(labels)
    total = len(labels)
    return -sum(
        (c / total) * log2(c / total)
        for c in counts.values()
    )

def information_gain(
    parent: list[str],
    splits: list[list[str]]
) -> float:
    total = len(parent)
    child_entropy = sum(
        (len(s) / total) * entropy(s)
        for s in splits
    )
    return entropy(parent) - child_entropy
```

#### The Connection

Much like a decision tree prunes irrelevant features to make better classifications, we prune irrelevant information to make better decisions. And just as over-pruning a tree loses useful signal, over-simplifying a decision loses important nuance. The ALAP principle Shane Parrish describes is essentially a human heuristic for knowing when to stop pruning.
